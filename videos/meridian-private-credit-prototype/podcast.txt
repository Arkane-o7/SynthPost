[SECTION:headline]
Corporate lending did not leave banking. It changed clothes. More loans now arrive through private credit funds, away from public markets.

[SECTION:fed_scale]
By late 2025, the Federal Reserve estimated private credit at 1.4 trillion dollars: 10 percent of U.S. corporate debt and roughly one-third of speculative-grade debt outside bank loans.

[SECTION:narrator_reaction]
That sounds like shadow banking. The awkward correction: many funds lock money up for years. No traditional deposit run—at least in theory.

[SECTION:mechanism]
Borrowers pay for certainty, speed, and custom covenants. One large loan can be negotiated with one lender instead of marketed to hundreds of bond investors.

[SECTION:imf_document]
The IMF says immediate stability risks look contained, but the structure is opaque. Borrowers are leveraged, rates float, and losses can hide because private loans lack a continuous market price.

[SECTION:social_twist]
Here is the twist: banks did not vanish. They lend to funds, provide subscription lines, and finance portfolios. Credit left the bank balance sheet; bank exposure remained connected.

[SECTION:capital_chain]
Now retail money is entering semi-liquid vehicles. The Fed counted 425 billion dollars in gross assets. Redemption windows bring liquidity pressure back into an asset class designed to avoid it.

[SECTION:footage]
Private credit is neither a safer bank replacement nor a hidden time bomb. It is a faster, quieter lending machine whose risks are harder to see.

[SECTION:thesis]
The trade is visibility for flexibility. That works beautifully—until everyone asks what the loan is worth at the same time.
